Does Size Matter?

Exploring whether a country's population size — and how fast it has grown — can explain why some nations are so much happier than others.

Small countries, big smiles

Look at any edition of the World Happiness Report and a pattern immediately jumps out: the countries at the top — Finland, Denmark, Iceland, Norway, New Zealand — are all relatively small nations. Is this a coincidence, or does it reflect something fundamental about the relationship between a society's size and its well-being?

This data story combines the World Happiness Report (2015–2022) with World Bank population data (1980–2022) to examine 162 countries across both dimensions. Happiness scores are averaged across eight years to smooth out year-to-year noise; population growth is measured as the percentage change since 1980.

Start with the map. Notice the deep blue across Northern Europe and the warm red across Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. The question we want to answer: is the colour of a country partly determined by how many people live there?

The happiest countries are strikingly small

The top 10 is almost entirely composed of Western European nations plus Australia and New Zealand — all with populations well under 50 million. Finland (≈5.5M), Iceland (≈370K), and Denmark (≈5.9M) lead the pack. The bottom 10 is dominated by Sub-Saharan African countries, many with populations in the tens of millions and growing rapidly.

Hover over any bar to see the country's population and GDP per capita. The contrast in size is striking — though it immediately raises the question of whether geography and wealth, not size alone, are driving the difference.

A modest, but real, size effect

Put all 162 countries on a single chart and a downward trend emerges: countries with larger populations tend to score slightly lower on happiness. The x-axis uses a log scale because population ranges from 370,000 (Iceland) to 1.4 billion (China) — a linear axis would compress most countries into a sliver on the left.

But look at the spread. The USA (330M) scores above 7; many small African nations score below 4. Colouring by region reveals that a large part of the pattern is geographic — Sub-Saharan African countries are both large-growth nations and low-happiness nations, while Western European countries are both small and happy. Is it size that matters, or something that happens to correlate with size?

It is not just size — it is trajectory

Instead of asking how big a country is, what if we ask how fast it has changed? The chart below plots each country's percentage population growth from 1980 to 2022 against its average happiness score. The negative relationship is noticeably clearer.

Countries in Sub-Saharan Africa and the Middle East have seen 200–800% population growth and tend to score low on happiness. Eastern European countries cluster on the left with shrinking populations — a reflection of post-Soviet emigration — and score around the middle of the scale.

Note: UAE, Qatar, and Saudi Arabia are excluded from this chart. Their extreme growth (800–900%) is driven almost entirely by labour migration rather than natural population growth, which would distort the trend line without adding meaningful insight.

What actually drives happiness?

Population size and growth tell part of the story — but a small part. Use the dropdown below to explore all features in the dataset. Switch to GDP per Capita, Social Support, or Healthy Life Expectancy and watch: the weak negative relationship disappears and a clear, tight positive relationship emerges.

This raises a deeper question. Rapidly growing countries tend to have both low GDP and low social support. So is it population growth that makes people unhappy — or do poverty and weak institutions simply happen to coexist with rapid demographic change? The data cannot resolve causality, but it strongly suggests that improving living conditions is more likely to raise happiness than controlling population size.

What does it all mean?

We started with a simple observation: the world's happiest countries are small. That observation holds up — there is a statistically significant negative relationship between population size and happiness, and an even stronger one between population growth and happiness.

But the headline finding is what we don't find. Once you look at GDP per capita, social support, and healthy life expectancy, the population effect shrinks considerably. The countries that are both populous and unhappy are also the countries with weak institutions, low incomes, and poor health infrastructure. The countries that are both small and happy are also the countries with strong safety nets, high trust, and long lives.

Small countries tend to be happier — but it is their wealth, social cohesion, and institutions that drive the difference, not their size alone. Population growth is a stronger signal than size, but both are dwarfed by the structural factors that separate thriving societies from struggling ones.

In other words: size may matter a little — but what your society offers its citizens matters a lot more.
