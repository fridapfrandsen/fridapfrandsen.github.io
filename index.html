Does Size
Matter?

An exploration of whether a country's population size — and how fast it has grown — can explain why some nations are so much happier than others.

Introduction Exploration Insights Conclusion 📓 Explainer Notebook
01 / Introduction

Small countries, big smiles

Look at any edition of the World Happiness Report and a pattern immediately jumps out: the countries at the very top — Finland, Denmark, Iceland, Norway, New Zealand — are all relatively small nations. Is this a coincidence, or does it reflect something fundamental about the relationship between a society's size and its well-being?

This data story investigates that question. We combine the World Happiness Report (2015–2022) with World Bank population data (1980–2022) to track 162 countries across both dimensions. Happiness scores are averaged across the eight-year period to smooth out year-to-year noise. Population growth is measured as the percentage change from 1980 to 2022.

The map below is your starting point. It immediately reveals the broad geography of happiness — a deep blue Northern Europe, a warm red Africa and South Asia. The question we want to answer: is the colour of a country partly determined by how many people live there?

02 / Exploration

The happiest countries are strikingly small

Let's zoom in on the concrete countries. The chart below shows the top 10 happiest and bottom 10 least happy countries in our dataset. Hover over any bar to reveal the country's population — the contrast is striking.

The top 10 is almost entirely composed of Western European nations plus Australia and New Zealand, all with populations well under 50 million. Finland (≈5.5M), Iceland (≈370K), and Denmark (≈5.9M) lead the pack. The bottom 10 is dominated by Sub-Saharan African countries — many with populations in the tens of millions and growing rapidly.

Figure 2: Top 10 and Bottom 10 countries by average happiness score (2015–2022). Hover to see population size and GDP per capita.

The pattern is suggestive — but could it simply reflect geography rather than size? Western European countries are both small and wealthy. To separate these effects, we need to look at all 162 countries at once and ask: once you put every country on a chart of size versus happiness, does a downward trend emerge?

03 / Key Insights

It's not just size — it's trajectory

01

Size has a weak negative link

Larger populations tend to score slightly lower on happiness (r ≈ −0.24), but the relationship is modest and far from deterministic.

02

Growth is a stronger signal

Countries that have grown fastest since 1980 are on average less happy (r ≈ −0.40). Rapid demographic change seems to coincide with lower well-being.

03

GDP & social support dominate

Once you control for GDP per capita, social support, and life expectancy (all r > 0.7), the population effect largely disappears. Size may be a symptom, not the cause.

Happiness vs. population size

Each point in the chart below is one country. The x-axis uses a log scale because population ranges from 370,000 (Iceland) to 1.4 billion (China) — a linear axis would compress most countries into a tiny sliver on the left.

The dashed regression line shows a modest downward slope: countries with larger populations tend to score slightly lower. But look at the spread — the USA (330M) scores above 7, while many small African nations score below 4. Colour by region makes it clear that a big part of the pattern is geographic: Sub-Saharan African countries are both large-growth nations and low-happiness nations, while Western European countries are both small and happy.

Figure 3: Happiness score vs. population size (log scale). Each point is one country, coloured by region. The dashed line shows the OLS trend.

Happiness vs. population growth

Now we shift the question: instead of asking how big a country is, we ask how fast it has changed. The chart below plots each country's percentage population growth from 1980 to 2022 against its average happiness score.

The negative relationship is clearer here. Countries in Sub-Saharan Africa and the Middle East have seen 200–800% population growth and tend to score low on happiness. Eastern European countries cluster on the left with shrinking populations — a reflection of post-Soviet emigration — and score around the middle of the scale.

Note: UAE, Qatar, and Saudi Arabia are excluded from this chart. Their extreme growth (800–900%) is driven almost entirely by labour migration rather than natural population growth, which would distort the regression line without adding meaningful insight.

Figure 4: Happiness score vs. population growth 1980–2022. UAE, Qatar and Saudi Arabia are excluded (growth driven by labour migration, not births).

What actually drives happiness?

Population size and growth tell part of the story — but a small part. The interactive chart below lets you explore all the features in the dataset. Try switching to GDP per Capita, Social Support, or Healthy Life Expectancy: the negative relationship disappears and a clear, tight positive relationship emerges instead (r > 0.7). These structural factors — not population — are the real engines of national well-being.

This raises a deeper question: rapidly growing countries tend to have both low GDP and low social support. So is it population growth that makes people unhappy, or do poverty and weak institutions simply happen to coexist with rapid demographic change? The data cannot resolve causality — but it strongly suggests that improving living conditions is more likely to raise happiness than controlling population size.

Figure 5: Use the dropdown to compare happiness against any feature in the dataset. GDP per capita, social support, and life expectancy are far stronger predictors of happiness than population size or growth.

04 / Conclusion

What does it all mean?

We started with a simple observation: the world's happiest countries are small. That observation holds up — there is a statistically significant negative relationship between population size and happiness, and an even stronger one between population growth and happiness. Countries that have expanded fastest since 1980 are, on average, markedly less happy than those that have remained stable or shrunk.

But the headline finding is what we don't find: population is not the main driver. Once you look at GDP per capita, social support, and healthy life expectancy, the population effect becomes much smaller. The countries that are both populous and unhappy are also the countries with weak institutions, low incomes, and poor health infrastructure. The countries that are both small and happy are also the countries with strong safety nets, high trust, and long lives.

In other words: size may matter a little — but what your society offers its citizens matters a lot more.

💡

In short: Small countries tend to be happier — but it is their wealth, social cohesion, and institutions that drive the difference, not their size alone. Population growth is a stronger signal than size, but both are dwarfed by the structural factors that separate thriving societies from struggling ones.
